Fireflies

Some firefly species in Southeast Asia synchronize their flashing across an entire tree, with no leader and no signal telling them all to start at once. Each insect just watches its neighbors and nudges its own rhythm toward theirs, and that's enough for the whole group to fall into a shared beat.

the rule

Every firefly blinks on the same period, but each has its own phase offset, which is where in the cycle it happens to be. A firefly's brightness follows a wave shaped by its offset: mostly dark, with one glowing pulse per period. A blink is the moment that pulse peaks.

how they sync

When a firefly blinks, every other firefly within vision radius nudges its own phase offset toward it, by an amount set by sync strength. reading the glow

Each blink has a sharp attack (fast brighten) and a softer decay (slower fade). Real firefly flashes turn on quicker than they fade, which is part of what gives a single blink its shape against the dark. the sync number

The number in the top left is the kuramoto order parameter, the standard way to measure how in-sync a crowd of oscillators is. Picture every firefly's phase offset as an arrow pointing out from the center of a clock face. As time passes every arrow sweeps around together at the real period. The dotted line marks $\theta = 0$, and an arrow lights up with the real attack/decay shape whenever it crosses it, exactly like an actual blink. The solid triangle is the average of all the others; it sweeps around with them, but its length never changes. Above each diagram is that same group as an actual population of fireflies:

The thin arrows are the individual fireflies; the solid triangle is their average. Scattered arrows cancel out to almost nothing ($0$); aligned arrows add up to nearly full length ($1$), and that length is the order parameter.
