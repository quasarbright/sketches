Maze Solver

Pick a way to solve the maze and watch it work. Every cell it looks at flares white-hot and cools to a faint afterglow, so the order it searched in stays on screen, and the route it found lights up in amber.

searching blind

The simplest searches don't use where the goal is. Breadth-first search looks at every cell one step from the start, then every cell two steps away, and so on, so it spreads out in rings. The first ring to reach the goal gives a shortest route.

Depth-first search picks a passage and follows it as far as it goes, backing up only at a dead end. It can get lucky or wander the whole maze, and the route it finds can be a long way round.

aiming at the goal

A* uses where the goal is. It always looks next at the cell where the distance walked so far plus the straight distance still to go is smallest. It still finds a shortest route, but it only drifts away from the goal when a wall forces it to, so it looks at far fewer cells.

Greedy best-first keeps only the distance still to go. It heads straight for the goal, which is fast in an open maze, but dead ends fool it and its route isn't always the shortest. Bidirectional search spreads in rings from both ends at once, cyan from the start and pink from the goal, and stops where they meet.

That maze has loops, which is where the difference shows. With no loops there's only one route, so every method finds the same one, and the only question is how much it looks at on the way.

walking it

The rest solve it the way you would on foot, seeing only where you're standing. The wall follower keeps its right hand on the wall. In a maze without loops that always gets you out eventually; with loops it can circle an island forever.

Trémaux's method marks every passage it walks. At a junction it takes an unmarked passage, it never walks one a third time, and when a new passage leads somewhere it's already been, it turns straight back. It works on any maze. The random mouse picks a random way at every step, which on a big maze takes a very long time.

For a walker, the amber route is its walk with every loop it went round cut out.

filling dead ends

Dead-end filling doesn't walk at all. A cell with only one way in or out can't be on the route (unless it's the start or the goal), so it gets sealed off, which can turn its neighbor into a dead end too. Keep sealing, and in a maze without loops only the route is left.

the mazes

Winding corridors are carved by a walk that keeps going until it's boxed in, then backs up to the last place with an unvisited neighbor: long passages and few branches. Short branches grow outward from everywhere at once, so dead ends are short and everywhere. The loops slider knocks out extra walls afterward.
