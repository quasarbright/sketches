welcome to shmorbit

An orbital mechanics sandbox with no rocket — just a ship, the real solar system, and Kepler. Everything counterintuitive about orbits lives here: to catch up to something, slow down; to go faster at the far side, burn at the near side. Sizes, masses, and orbits are the real ones (flattened to a plane), so a Mars transfer really costs ~3.6 km/s and really takes five months.

the model

Motion is one two-body problem at a time. Near Earth you're on a conic (ellipse or hyperbola) around Earth; inside the Moon's sphere of influence — the region where its gravity dominates — you're on a conic around the Moon, and past Earth's own sphere of influence you're on an orbit around the Sun. The path is these conics patched together at the boundaries. Each conic obeys vis-viva, $v^2 = \mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)$, and is solved in closed form: no simulation steps, just Kepler's equation. Maneuvers are instant velocity changes that fire automatically at their planned time.

why time travel is free

Because everything is closed-form, your position is a pure function of the clock and the mission plan. Warp, rewind, and jump don't fast-forward a simulation — they just move the clock. Rewinding to before a maneuver un-executes it, and the future re-forms the moment you edit the plan.

The one thing that must be hunted for rather than solved for is where your path first dips into the Moon's sphere of influence, so the future is only surveyed a few weeks ahead. A long warp surveys as it goes — and if it turns up an encounter your map didn't show, the warp stops just before it.

flying
  • Click your trajectory to place a maneuver or warp to that point; maneuvers snap to Ap/Pe.
  • Drag the yellow handles for prograde/retrograde, cyan for radial. Hold shift while dragging for fine control.
  • Select the Moon as target to see your closest approach; raise your orbit until the paths kiss.
  • Scroll to zoom, drag to pan; the camera stays on the ship.
