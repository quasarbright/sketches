Perspectiven't

A camera decides how big things look from how far away they are. A normal camera divides by distance. This lets you swap that rule for any function you like and walk around inside the result.

how a normal camera works

Light from a point in the world travels in a straight line to your eye. The picture is where those lines cross a screen in front of you. A thing twice as far away needs a line that's twice as shallow to reach the eye, so it lands half as far from the center: on the screen, a point's offset is its real offset divided by its distance $z$.

Rays fanning out from the eye. Everything on one ray lands on the same spot.

a plane behind the screen

The lines don't have to start at a single point. Start them from a plane at the eye instead, and send each one through the matching spot on the screen. When the plane shrinks to nothing you have the normal camera. When it's as big as the screen the rays are parallel, nothing shrinks with distance, and you get an orthographic view, the kind used in technical drawings. Make the plane bigger than the screen and the rays converge again, this time in front of you, so far things look bigger. That's reverse perspective, and nothing past the meeting point can be seen at all.

The plane is always centered on your eye, so your eye stays in the same place whichever lens you pick. Its lower part reaches below your feet, and rays that start there are inside the earth, so they see solid ground. Looking straight ahead in orthographic, the ground's surface is edge-on, and walking forward changes nothing until you pass something, because distance no longer changes size. Step sideways or look down to see the world move.

Following one ray: it starts at $s\,u$ on a plane $s$ times the screen's size and reaches $u$ at the screen distance $D$, so at depth $z$ it's at $u\,(s + (1-s)\,z/D)$. Dividing by that is the same as dividing by $f(z) = sD + (1-s)\,z$. As $s$ slides from 0 to 1, $f$ slides from $z$ to a constant, which is how the camera can morph smoothly from perspective to orthographic instead of cutting between them.

any function of distance

Once the camera is "divide by $f(z)$", nothing says $f$ has to be a straight line. Divide by $\sqrt z$ and things shrink more slowly than normal. Divide by $z^2$ and they shrink faster. Divide by $1/z$, which is multiplying by distance, and the world turns inside out. The reverse button uses $1/(z + 5)$ instead: its rays start on a plane at your eye a couple of times bigger than the screen and bend in toward each other, meeting only infinitely far away. Plain $1/z$ would need that plane to be infinitely big. The rays aren't straight any more, and straight edges in the world come out curved.

Everything is scaled so that things at the screen distance keep their size, which is why switching lenses doesn't make the thing you're looking at jump. Where $f$ is zero or negative, no ray reaches, so that part of the world disappears.

two lenses at once

Side-to-side and up-down positions are divided separately, so they can use different functions. Orthographic across and perspective up and down makes the rails stay parallel while the telegraph poles still shrink.

drawing it

Each corner of every shape goes through $f$ on the graphics card, and the shapes are cut into small pieces so the curves come out smooth. For the plane-behind-the-screen family, the math is an ordinary projection matrix, so those views are exact.
