Perspectiven't

A normal camera draws far things smaller, and exactly how much smaller comes from one division. Take a point in the world and measure how far it is above, below or beside the line you're looking along: its offset. The camera divides that offset by how far ahead of you the point is, and the answer is how far from the middle of the picture it's drawn. Twice as far ahead, it lands half as far from the middle, so a pole twice as far away is drawn half as tall. Here you can divide the offset by any function of the distance ahead, $f(z)$, instead of the distance $z$ itself, and walk around inside the result.

how a picture gets made

A picture is a grid of pixels, and every pixel gets its color the same way: a ray goes out from your eye in that pixel's direction, and the pixel takes the color of the first thing the ray hits. The rays fan out evenly between the top pixel's ray and the bottom pixel's. The angle between those two is the spread. what gets divided

Tracing a ray for every pixel is slow, so the game works the other way around. It takes each corner of each shape and works out which spot in the picture it lands on. Because the rays fan out evenly, $z$ meters ahead they cover a band of heights that grows in proportion to $z$: with a 58° spread, the top ray is $0.55\,z$ meters above the line you're looking along. A corner $y$ meters above that line (negative for below) is on the ray whose place in the picture is $y \div 0.55\,z$, where 1 is the top edge, 0 the middle and −1 the bottom. Its distance to the left or right is divided by $z$ the same way. That's the division: a corner's offset from the line you're looking along, divided by how far ahead it is.

A corner lands on exactly the pixel whose ray goes through it, so the two ways give the same picture. Every shape is made of triangles, so once the corners are placed, the graphics card fills in the triangles between them, nearest in front. That's how the game draws every frame. Below, each box corner is joined to your eye by the ray it's on, and placed in the picture by dividing.

a sensor and a spread

The rays don't have to start at one point. Give them a blue sensor to start from instead, a rectangle centered where your eye was, and start each pixel's ray at that pixel's spot on it. That's the sensor size slider. At zero it's a point, your eye. The spread slider still sets the angle between the top and bottom rays.

With a sensor you can set the spread to 0. Then every ray runs straight ahead, parallel to the rest, so nothing shrinks with distance. That's an orthographic view, the kind used in technical drawings. (From a point with no spread, every pixel would get the same ray, so the spread stops above 0 while the sensor is a point.)

The same scene from a sensor 2.2 m tall, with no spread. The far blue box keeps its real size.

The same with parallel rays from the sensor. You see the cube's sides but never its top.

Make the spread negative and the rays come together in front of you, so far things are drawn bigger than near ones. That's reverse perspective. Past the point where they cross, the rays carry on crossed, so out there the world is upside down.

The same sweep in 3D, and the picture it makes.

The division still works. With a sensor reaching $A$ meters above the line you're looking along and a spread of $2\phi$, the top ray is $A + z\tan\phi$ meters above that line $z$ meters ahead, so a corner $y$ meters above it lands at $y \div (A + z\tan\phi)$ of the way to the top edge. That's the corner's offset divided by how spread out the rays are at its distance. Call that $f(z)$. For an eye it's $0.55\,z$, a multiple of $z$. With no spread it's the constant $A$, so a corner's place doesn't depend on its distance at all. Sliding the sensor and spread between the two moves $f$ smoothly from one to the other, which is how the view morphs from perspective to orthographic instead of cutting between them.

The sensor is centered on your eye, so you stay in the same place whichever lens you pick. Looking straight ahead in orthographic, the ground is edge-on, and walking forward changes nothing until you pass something, because distance no longer changes size. Step sideways or look down to see the world move.

any function of distance

Once the picture is "offset divided by $f(z)$", $f$ doesn't have to be a straight line. The pixel at height $u$ in the picture sees the points with $y = u\,f(z)$, and when $f$ bends, that ray is a curve. Divide the offset by $\sqrt z$ and things shrink more slowly than normal. Divide by $z^2$ and they shrink faster. Divide by $1/z$, which is multiplying by distance, and the world turns inside out. The reverse button uses $1/(z + 5)$ instead: its rays start on a big sensor and bend in toward each other, meeting only infinitely far away.

The curved rays, and the picture traced along them pixel by pixel.

With curved rays, straight edges in the world come out curved. The graphics card can only fill flat triangles between corners, so the game cuts every shape into small pieces and places each piece's corners, and the edges bend. Each lens is scaled so that things about 2.4 m ahead keep their size, which is why switching lenses doesn't make the thing you're looking at jump. Where $f$ is zero or negative, no ray gets there, so that part of the world disappears. With curved rays the spread slider zooms.

two lenses at once

The offset up or down and the offset left or right are divided separately, so they can use different functions. Orthographic horizontally and perspective vertically keeps the rails parallel while the telegraph poles still shrink.
