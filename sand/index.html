Sand

Paint materials and watch them fall, flow, burn and dissolve. The screen is a grid of cells, each holding at most one grain, and every step each grain follows a few rules about the cells right next to it. Everything else, from slopes to puddles to a forest fire, comes out of those rules.

everything at once

The obvious way to run this is one grain at a time: sand looks below itself and moves down if the cell is empty. That's how the older version worked, 10,000 cells in a loop. This one runs on the graphics card, which computes every cell of the next step at the same time, each from a snapshot of the last step. That's fast enough for a whole screen of grains, but it breaks the obvious rule. Two grains can both see the same empty cell and both move into it, and one of them disappears. The way around it is to make sure that, for every kind of move, each empty cell has only one grain that could fill it, and that the grain and the cell agree on it. Then the cell can pull its grain in while the grain's old cell lets it go, both working from the same snapshot. Each kind of move gets its own pass.

falling

A cell only ever takes the grain directly above it, so falling has one source per cell already. A grain falls when the grains under it are a stack that ends in an empty cell, and then the whole stack moves down together. If only the bottom grain moved, a falling clump would come apart into stripes, one row at a time. A stack that ends on stone, wood or the floor is held up, and only held-up grains slide or flow; otherwise a falling stream of water would spray out sideways in midair.

flowing

Every grain of liquid carries a direction, left or right. When it's held up, it moves one cell that way into an empty cell, and turns around when it bumps into something. Keeping a direction is what levels a puddle: a grain on top of a mound walks straight to the edge and falls off, where one that picked a new direction every time would wander back and forth. An empty cell between two grains heading toward it picks one of them at random, and both grains compute the same pick. Lava is thick, so it only flows one time in five. This pass runs six times per step, so liquids spread faster than things fall.

sliding, in 2×2 blocks

Diagonal slides are what give piles their slopes, and they're harder: a grain could come from the upper left or the upper right, and the cell beside it might be moving too. So for these, the grid is cut into blocks of 2×2 cells. A grain only slides within its own block, all four cells of a block work out the same rearrangement, and each keeps its own share. No other block touches those four cells, so nothing can be claimed twice. In a block, a held-up grain in the top row slides down into an empty cell diagonally below it, and sand sitting on a liquid swaps places with it, which is how sand sinks. On the next step the blocks shift one cell down and right, and then back, so grains can cross block edges. Each block is also flipped left-to-right at random, so a grain has no favorite side.

reacting to neighbors

Reactions are the last pass, and they need no blocks. A cell never changes another cell, only itself: it looks at its 8 neighbors and decides what it becomes. For every burning neighbor, wood has a 1% chance of catching fire, and gasoline and gunpowder 10%. For every acid neighbor, wood has a 10% chance of dissolving, sand 5% and stone 1%. Stone next to lava melts into more lava at 0.3% per neighbor.

Fire and lava also carry a countdown. Fire loses one tick for every neighbor that's empty or won't burn, so it lasts as long as there's fuel around it and dies in a few dozen steps in open air. Water puts it out at once. Lava counts down against everything that isn't fire or lava, except while it's falling, and turns to stone when it runs out, or the moment it touches water. Both get yellower as they run down.
